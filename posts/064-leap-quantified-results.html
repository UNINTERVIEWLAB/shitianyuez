NO.64 · CAPABILITY DEEPENING

Quantify the result you can defend

2026.11.25

Capabilities, JD mapping and objective

Knowledge Translation · Knowledge Management & Evidence · Uptake & Capacity Development. 25 November 2026 · 10–15 minutes.

JD connection: prepare communication products, training evidence and partnership reports. Objective: choose a meaningful number, define its scope and state what it does—and does not—demonstrate. This lesson deepens reporting judgement; it is not another introductory adoption pathway.

A number is not automatically a result

“Ten articles” describes output volume. “Two training rounds” describes delivery. “Approximately 20 participants” describes reach. Each can demonstrate scale, but none alone proves understanding, changed behaviour or environmental benefit.

A defensible result combines an observable change or completed output with a defined unit, period, source and contribution boundary. Quantification can describe timeliness, completion, quality or coverage; it need not invent a percentage improvement.

  • Scale: how many outputs, people or organisations? Avoid mixing people and attendance instances.
  • Quality: what review or acceptance criterion was met, and who checked it?
  • Timeliness: what agreed deadline was met? Do not infer this merely because an event happened.
  • Change: compared with what baseline, among whom, over what period?
  • Contribution: what did the individual do, and what belongs to the wider team or external conditions?

LEAP reporting scenario

For a hypothetical dissemination package supporting the feed-guideline revision, distinguish reviewed case notes, webinar attendance, assessed understanding, verified application and measured environmental performance. New ingredients or mitigation measures discussed in a webinar are not thereby proven to reduce emissions.

A reporting line could use blank fields: “[N] case notes were technically reviewed against [criterion] by [date], supporting [specified training product].” It should not become “the programme improved global feed sustainability” without evidence at that level.

Downloads show access activity, not unique readers or adoption unless the measurement establishes those units. A positive post-event survey describes respondents’ feedback, not every participant’s view. State the response count and denominator; identify self-report and non-response.

Scientific outcome claims require the relevant assessment method, boundary, baseline, data and uncertainty checks. Secretariat reporting should route such claims to authorised experts, not calculate benefits from attendance figures.

Experience mapping: use the right evidence level

Main case — Intellisia Institute: Your ten articles and institutional-content work provide a concrete output count. AI-governance research and forum preparation show the analytical and coordination work behind those products. To describe a stronger result, verify publication status, editorial acceptance or audience analytics with a defined period and metric. Do not reuse an unconfirmed readership-growth percentage or claim that publication caused policy change.

Comparison — FAO Rwanda: Two rounds of hydroponic/vertical-farming training for approximately 20 young participants establish activity scale. Your contribution concerned technical-resource coordination, participant communication and delivery. Practical learning can be described only to the extent supported by observation or assessment; continued adoption, income gains and environmental effects need follow-up evidence.

Transfer: both cases connect knowledge products with intended users, but their metrics measure different things. Article output is not training reach; training reach is not application. A useful LEAP report keeps these layers separate.

Boundary: you have evidence of research, communication and capacity-building coordination, not independent LCA validation or proven attribution of long-term environmental change.

Build a claim-evidence register

For each planned claim, record: exact wording; evidence type; unit; numerator/denominator where relevant; period; source/version; reviewer; personal contribution; limitation; and permitted public wording. Use “not measured” rather than filling a gap with a plausible number.

If only an output is verified, report the output clearly and explain its intended purpose separately. If a later outcome is documented, specify the evidence and avoid implying sole causation. Good reporting can be precise without sounding grand.

Five reusable expressions

  • quantify delivery without overstating downstream impact
  • define the unit, period and evidence source behind the number
  • distinguish respondent feedback from verified application
  • attribute the contribution at the appropriate level
  • report an evidence gap rather than manufacture precision

Private output · 5–10 minutes

Create a one-page Claim–Metric–Evidence Register with three rows: an Intellisia knowledge product; a FAO training activity; a hypothetical LEAP dissemination output. Columns: defensible claim; metric/unit; source and period; personal action; unsupported inference to avoid; next evidence needed.

Use only confirmed facts. Leave dates, analytics and quality checks blank when unknown. Rewrite one claim to be more precise—not more impressive. Keep completed answers and supporting private records off the public website.
